# Practice-Maintenance and the Auditor's Learning Cycle

## Motivation

AI audit is a practice at a moving boundary. Regulations are being written in real time: the EU AI Act's implementing acts, delegated acts, and standards are landing through 2026 and beyond; US state laws and federal guidance are shifting quarterly; ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 continues to publish and revise standards; sector regulators are rewriting their AI playbooks; the technical underpinnings (foundation models, agents, evaluation methodologies) are evolving faster than any of the above. A senior AI auditor who stops reading stops being competent; a function whose practice-maintenance is informal produces engagement work that was current six months ago and stale now.

Practice-maintenance is the discipline of staying current and keeping the function current. It is not optional — IIA Standards, ISO/IEC 17021-1, IRCA scheme requirements, ISQM 1, and GAGAS all require continuing professional development in different shapes. But the requirement is a floor, not a ceiling. The senior auditor's job is to run a continuous learning cycle for themselves and for the function that stays ahead of the external pace, not just abreast of it.

This chapter walks the sources the senior AI auditor subscribes to, the pattern for integrating what is learned, the function's own practice-maintenance routine, and the posture that treats the audit function as a living practice rather than a one-time build.

## The sources to subscribe to

### Professional-body journals and publications

- **ISACA Journal** — bimonthly; covers IT audit, governance, and increasingly AI-specific topics.
- **IIA Internal Auditor magazine** and **The IIA's Global Knowledge Briefs and Practice Guides** — practical guidance; the IIA's AI Auditing Framework and AI-specific guidance are issued here.
- **IAASB publications** — ISA, ISAE, ISRS, ISRE pronouncements and exposure drafts; the IAASB's work on technology-and-audit is relevant.
- **AICPA publications** — Journal of Accountancy; AICPA practice aids; SOC updates; forthcoming AI-specific attestation guidance.
- **CPA Canada** publications; **ICAEW** publications; equivalents in other jurisdictions where the function operates.
- **GAO** — Yellow Book updates; GAO AI Accountability Framework and related publications.
- **PCAOB** — standard-setting activity; staff guidance; inspection-report observations.

### Government and regulator rulemaking dockets

- **EU AI Office / European Commission** — implementing acts, delegated acts, guidance, Member State designations.
- **EU AI Board / EAIB** — coordination outputs.
- **US regulators** — AISI publications; FDA guidance; Fed SR letters; OCC bulletins; FDIC FILs; CFPB bulletins; FTC enforcement actions and policy statements; EEOC guidance; DOJ statements.
- **UK AISI, ICO, FCA, PRA, Ofcom, MHRA, CMA** publications.
- **State AG offices** — enforcement actions, guidance on state AI laws.
- **NAIC** — Model Bulletin updates, state adoption trackers.
- **Non-US regulators** — CNIL, ICO, Garante, BfDI, AEPD, DPC Ireland, IMDA / PDPC, PPC, PIPC, OAIC and their equivalents.

Rulemaking dockets (US Federal Register; EUR-Lex; UK legislation.gov.uk; each jurisdiction's equivalent) are the primary source for regulatory change. The function subscribes to the specific docket feeds where its audited entities operate.

### Academic and peer-reviewed work

- **FAccT (Fairness, Accountability, Transparency)** — annual conference proceedings.
- **AIES (AI, Ethics, and Society)** — annual conference proceedings.
- **NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR** — track safety and evaluation papers relevant to audit.
- **Communications of the ACM** and **IEEE Computer** — overview articles useful for staying current on technical shifts.
- **Nature Machine Intelligence, AI & Society, Minds and Machines** — peer-reviewed journals with policy and ethics content.

### Case law and enforcement actions

Enforcement actions are an underused source. Regulator enforcement actions (FTC consent decrees; CFPB orders; EU DPA enforcement decisions; state AG settlements; DOJ settlements and prosecutions; sector-regulator public actions) show what regulators treat as material breaches in practice. Case law on algorithmic decisioning, IP (AI training data), liability, and employment discrimination is accumulating; the function tracks the significant cases.

### Trade and practitioner communities

- **The IIA's AI Audit practice communities** and chapters; AICPA; ISACA; IRCA; the IAF accreditation-body world; professional bodies' AI special-interest groups.
- **Conferences** — IIA International Conference, ISACA events, AICPA & CIMA Governance & Risk Conferences, GRC Conference, OECD.AI events, FAccT, AI safety / governance conferences, sector-specific (medical device safety, banking MRM, insurance innovation).
- **Practitioner networks** — informal peer groups among AI auditors across organisations; reciprocity-based intelligence sharing.
- **Vendor-user communities** — for GRC-for-AI platforms the function uses, the vendor's user community is a practical source.

## Patterns for integrating what is learned

Subscription alone is noise. The senior auditor has a pattern for processing sources into practice.

### The weekly reading routine

- A defined time block each week (commonly 2–4 hours).
- A queue of sources prioritised by recency and relevance.
- Note-taking that captures: what the source is; what the material point is; what it changes for the function's practice; what to do about it.
- The notes feed into (a) immediate adjustments to current engagements, (b) backlog items for the function, (c) the committee's or CAE's awareness.

### The monthly practice review

- A short recurring meeting where the senior auditor or the function reviews what has changed in the external environment and what the function is going to do.
- Output: an updated practice-maintenance backlog; identified training needs; identified tooling needs; scope or scheduling decisions for upcoming engagements.

### The quarterly impact review

- Did the function act on last quarter's learnings?
- Did engagements reflect the current state of criteria and practice?
- Did the function's output anticipate or lag the regulatory environment?

### The annual practice retrospective

- What did the function do well this year? What would it do differently?
- Did the practice-maintenance effort produce measurable improvement in engagement quality?
- Where did the function get caught flat-footed?

## The function's training programme

Beyond the senior auditor's personal practice, the function has a training programme:

### Onboarding

New team members onboard on:

- The function's charter, policies, procedures, and tooling.
- The IIA Standards and the engagement-scheme standards relevant to the function's work.
- The organisation's AIMS and AI risk framework.
- The team's workpaper templates and conventions.
- Current engagements.
- The organisation's AI inventory.
- Safety training on AI incidents and dual-use risk.

### Continuing education

- **Certification maintenance.** CPE / CPD hours tracked for CIA, CISA, CRISC, CFE, CPA, CA, IRCA-registered auditors, scheme-specific certifications. The IIA requires 40 CPE hours annually for CIA; ISACA requires 120 CPE hours over three years for CISA with a minimum annual requirement.<!-- needs-research: confirm current CPE / CPD requirements for each certification at reading time. -->
- **Specialist training.** AI-specific courses — model risk, fairness, security, evaluation, ISO/IEC 42001 lead auditor training (IRCA-certified), AI Act training.
- **Skills training.** Technical skills (SQL, Python, OSCAL, evaluation frameworks), communication skills, interviewing, negotiation.
- **Shadowing.** Junior auditors shadow senior auditors on engagements; senior auditors shadow externals where possible.
- **Rotation.** Team members rotate across engagement types to build breadth.

### Training budget

Documented; committed; defended in the function's budget conversation with the CAE and the audit committee. A function that does not fund training produces stale output.

## The function's own maturity cycle

The function applies maturity thinking to itself.

### Dimensions

- **Audit coverage** — what portion of the organisation's AI footprint the function covers each cycle.
- **Engagement quality** — adherence to the standard; findings-slate robustness; peer-review outcomes.
- **Continuous-audit coverage** — what portion of the function's coverage runs through continuous audit vs point-in-time engagements.
- **Tooling maturity** — the tooling layer from chapter 4.
- **Team capability** — skills and certification coverage.
- **External reliance posture** — the external auditors' ability to rely on the function's work.
- **Regulator-engagement maturity** — the function's track record with regulators.

### Annual self-assessment

The function self-assesses on each dimension with evidence. The self-assessment goes to the CAE and the audit committee as part of the annual report.

### External validation

- IIA External Quality Assessment every five years (and ongoing internal assessments).
- Peer reviews with other AI audit functions (reciprocal).
- Publication of practice (where appropriate) — contributing to IIA / ISACA / standards bodies feeds back credibility.

## The posture — the audit function as a living practice

The audit function's product is trust: the audit committee's trust in the function; the organisation's trust in the function's independence; the regulator's and external auditor's trust in the function's output. Trust is built by consistent, defensible, current practice. It erodes quickly when the practice slips.

A living practice means:

- Methods are revised when sources justify revision.
- Templates are updated quarterly as standards evolve.
- Tooling is maintained like production software.
- Team learning is funded, tracked, and visible.
- The function publishes its practice (internally and, where appropriate, externally) so others can review and challenge.
- The function is willing to say "we got this wrong last year, here is what we have changed" — a function that cannot be wrong cannot improve.
- Succession is planned — the senior auditor's knowledge is in the function, not in a person; documentation, templates, decision logs, workpaper trails, and shadowing are the vehicles.

## A note on AI as a tool of the audit function itself

The AI audit function increasingly uses AI tools in its own work — document review, anomaly detection, sampling optimisation, workpaper summarisation. The same disciplines the function expects of management apply to the function:

- **Tool governance.** Audit's AI tools are inventoried, assessed, approved.
- **Explainability.** The function can defend conclusions reached with AI-tool assistance.
- **Reliance posture.** The function does not rely on AI-tool output for sufficiency without human review.
- **Independence.** The function does not use AI tools that would compromise independence (tools provided by the auditee that could shape what the function sees).
- **Error handling.** When an AI tool produces a wrong result that reaches a report, the function corrects the record transparently.

This is a specific chapter of practice-maintenance — the auditor who uses the thing they audit is held to the same standard they hold others to.

## A short rubric for the senior auditor's own practice-maintenance

- Did I read my sources this week?
- Did I process the reading into notes that I can action?
- Did any current engagement need adjustment based on this week's reading?
- Have I contributed to the function's backlog?
- Did I attend a peer conversation, conference session, or committee working group this quarter?
- Is my CPE / CPD on track?
- Have I observed a junior auditor on an engagement and provided coaching?
- Have I been observed by a senior peer and received coaching?
- Did I publish or present on the function's practice this year?
- Do I know what will change in the external environment in the next 90 days that affects my next engagement?

A yes across this list is the standard.

## Summary

- AI audit is a practice at a moving boundary; practice-maintenance is a continuous discipline, not a credential-renewal event.
- Sources to subscribe to span professional-body journals (ISACA, IIA, IAASB, AICPA), regulator rulemaking dockets (EU AI Office, US AISI, FDA, FTC, EEOC, sector regulators, state regulators, non-US regulators), standards-body outputs (ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42, CEN/CENELEC JTC 21, NIST, IEEE, national standards bodies), industry bodies (Frontier Model Forum, Partnership on AI, MLCommons, OECD, UNESCO), academic and peer-reviewed work, enforcement actions and case law, and trade / practitioner communities.
- Integration patterns — weekly reading routine, monthly practice review, quarterly impact review, annual retrospective — turn subscription into practice change rather than noise.
- The function's training programme — onboarding, continuing education, specialist training, skills training, shadowing, rotation, and budget — sustains team capability.
- The function applies maturity thinking to itself across coverage, quality, continuous-audit, tooling, team, external reliance, and regulator-engagement dimensions, with external validation through IIA EQA, peer reviews, and publication.
- A living practice revises methods, updates templates, maintains tooling, funds and tracks learning, publishes practice where appropriate, admits error, and plans succession.
- AI tools used by the function itself are held to the same discipline — inventoried, assessed, approved, explainable, reliance-bounded, independence-preserving, error-handled transparently.
- The senior auditor's personal practice-maintenance rubric is a weekly discipline that sustains the function's trust over time.
