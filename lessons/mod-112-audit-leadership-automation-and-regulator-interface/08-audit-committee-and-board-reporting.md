# Audit-Committee and Board Reporting

## Motivation

The audit committee is the audit function's governance counterpart. The function's independence rests on the committee's authority; the function's credibility is built in how the committee experiences it; the function's influence runs through how well the committee can act on what it hears. For AI audits specifically, the committee is often working at the edge of its existing expertise — the AI subject matter is new to many audit-committee members, the regulatory landscape is evolving faster than the committee's own cadence, and the risk picture has implications the committee has not seen before.

Reporting to this audience is a craft. Members of audit committees read dozens of papers across their engagement with the organisation; the papers compete for attention and the papers have to work in constrained time. A report that is unstructured, buries the material point, or sprawls into technical detail is ignored. A report that leads with the decision the committee has to make, backs it with trend and evidence, and hands the committee the ability to challenge intelligently is the shape the committee recalls and acts on.

This chapter walks the standard reporting shapes — pre-read, one-page summary, decision log, risk heatmap, dashboard, repeat-finding read, maturity-model trend — and how the AI audit function uses them. It also covers the interface with the head of AI governance as a reporting audience.

## Where audit-committee reporting sits in the standards

- **IIA Global Internal Audit Standards** require the CAE to communicate with the board and senior management; the standards cover reporting responsibilities including periodic reporting on audit activity and significant risk and control issues.<!-- needs-research: cite current Domain IV / V Standard numbering at reading time; prior standards 2020 (Communication and Approval), 2060 (Reporting to Senior Management and the Board) have been consolidated. -->
- **NYSE and Nasdaq listing rules** require audit committees of listed companies to have specific authority and to meet with internal audit periodically.
- **PCAOB AS 1301 (Communications with Audit Committees)** governs external-auditor communications with audit committees; the internal function's work surfaces here as part of the external auditor's communication.
- **ISO/IEC 42001** addresses top-management responsibilities including review and communication; the audit committee is typically within "top management" for AIMS governance purposes.
- **Jurisdictional corporate-governance codes** (UK Corporate Governance Code, FRC AQR, OECD Principles of Corporate Governance, local equivalents) address audit-committee responsibilities and reporting expectations.

The senior AI auditor operates against the local code plus the IIA Standards as the governing baseline.

## The cadence

A typical pattern:

- **Four quarterly audit-committee meetings** with the full function on the agenda.
- **Monthly interim communication** (status note, dashboard, hot-item escalation).
- **Executive session** with the CAE and the audit committee without management present (typically at every quarterly meeting or on request).
- **Private communication to the chair** — direct access to the audit-committee chair the CAE can use between meetings for sensitive matters.
- **Annual audit-committee-only session** on the function's charter, plan, budget, and performance.

The AI audit function's reporting slots into this cadence. The function has at least one quarterly agenda slot and typically additional slots when AI-related engagements have significant findings or when AI-related risk has shifted materially.

## The pre-read

Each meeting has a pre-read sent in advance. For the AI audit portion:

### What goes in

- **Cover memo** (1 page) — what the committee is being asked to read, know, decide, or challenge.
- **Dashboard** (1–2 pages) — the core quantitative picture (see below).
- **Engagement summaries** (1 page each) — what each engagement completed this quarter concluded, with reference to the full report.
- **Significant findings** (as many pages as needed, but each finding tight) — new findings this quarter at material classification.
- **Status of open findings and corrective actions** (table).
- **AI risk landscape** (1 page) — changes in regulation, incidents in the external environment, emerging risks the function is watching.
- **Resource and plan status** (1 page) — hours spent vs plan, team utilisation, upcoming engagements.
- **Appendices** (as needed) — full report references, external-regulator correspondence, external-auditor coordination notes.

### What does not go in

- Methodology for methodology's sake.
- Workpaper excerpts without context.
- Technical detail that is not necessary for the committee to act.
- Pre-finalised findings (findings are briefed only once agreed with management).

### Preparation discipline

The pre-read is reviewed by the CAE before issue. Numbers reconcile to the source of truth. Prior-quarter statements are consistent with current statements (or differences are called out). The pre-read reflects the finalised audit record, not a draft.

## The one-page summary

Audit committees read the one-page summary first; many members read only the one-page summary. The structure:

### Suggested shape

**Title:** AI Audit — Quarter / Year summary

**The decision:** what the committee is being asked to do (approve the updated plan; acknowledge the findings; direct management on a specific risk; endorse the function's resourcing request).

**What has happened this quarter.** 3–5 bullets, factual:

- Engagements completed.
- Findings raised.
- Regulatory developments material to the audit scope.
- External-auditor coordination highlights.
- Resource status.

**What the committee should know.** 2–3 bullets, oriented to the committee's role:

- The most material finding and what it means.
- The most significant change in AI risk posture.
- Anything escalated to the committee's attention.

**What is coming next quarter.** 3–5 bullets, forward-looking:

- Engagements scheduled.
- Risk areas under active examination.
- Known regulator engagements.
- Resource needs.

**Trend.** A single sentence or small-multiple visualisation on the overall posture — improving / stable / deteriorating with a short "because" phrase.

Everything else is appendix.

## The decision log

Audit committees make decisions. The decisions can become hard to track across quarters, especially for AI which moves fast. The function maintains a decision log:

- Date of meeting.
- Decision.
- Who proposed.
- Who seconded (where formal).
- Who dissented (where recorded).
- Follow-up action.
- Follow-up status.

The log is presented each quarter as a status item: decisions made last quarter, action taken, outstanding decisions. The committee sees that its decisions are being actioned and can reopen decisions where circumstances have changed.

## The risk heatmap read

Many audit committees consume a risk heatmap — a visual of risk likelihood × impact across the organisation's risk categories. The AI-specific heatmap slots into the committee's broader enterprise risk picture.

For AI, the useful dimensions:

- **Risk categories** — regulatory, operational, financial-reporting, safety, fundamental-rights, reputational, security, third-party, and the categories the organisation's risk taxonomy uses.
- **Current residual risk** — likelihood and impact after controls.
- **Target residual risk** — what the organisation's risk appetite accepts.
- **Change from last quarter** — arrow / colour change.
- **Trigger events** — new regulation, new system, incident, loss event, control failure.

The audit function's heatmap is usually not the ERM function's; the audit function presents its own view and reconciles differences with the ERM view explicitly. Where the two views diverge materially, the committee is told.

## The dashboard

The audit-committee dashboard gives the committee a current picture of the audit function's output. For AI audit:

### Core panels

- **Plan execution.** Hours spent vs planned; engagements completed vs planned; engagements deferred or added; variance by major category.
- **Findings slate.** New findings this quarter by classification; open findings by age; closed findings this quarter.
- **Repeat findings.** Findings that have recurred across cycles — a key signal.
- **Continuous-audit exception trend.** Exception volume by severity; alert-to-finding ratio; new rules deployed.
- **Regulator engagement.** Active regulator examinations; recent completed examinations with outcomes; findings issued by regulators; remediation status.
- **External-auditor coordination.** Reliance planned by external auditors; coverage of their reliance needs by internal engagements.
- **Resource picture.** Headcount vs plan; specialist availability; training completion; certification status.

### What the dashboard does not do

- Does not grade management on its performance (that is an ERM or management function).
- Does not include predictions the function cannot defend.
- Does not combine incommensurable scales into a single composite score without explanation.

### Consistency across quarters

The same dashboard structure quarter after quarter, with the same scales and definitions, so the committee can read trend. Dashboard redesigns are communicated explicitly.

## The repeat-finding read

Repeat findings are the single most important signal the audit function surfaces to the audit committee. A finding that recurred across two cycles indicates the first remediation did not address the cause; a finding that recurred across three or more cycles indicates a systemic issue the committee has not yet mobilised to address.

### Shape of the repeat-finding read

- Finding ID and title.
- Cycles observed.
- Classification each cycle.
- Prior CAP commitments.
- What was done.
- Why the finding returned.
- Structural cause (per cause-analysis — chapter 1 of `mod-111`).
- Current recommendation.
- Management's current response.
- The committee's prior view (where recorded).

The repeat-finding read invites the committee's intervention — redirecting management's priorities, asking for a different executive owner, requiring outside support, or formally escalating the risk. This is where the audit committee can materially change the organisation's posture.

## The maturity-model trend

Audit committees are often interested in a view of the organisation's overall AI governance maturity over time. The function can present a maturity-model trend using a defensible rubric.

### Possible maturity models

- **NIST AI RMF implementation maturity** — the function assesses the organisation's RMF implementation against each function (GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE) and sub-function.
- **ISO/IEC 42001 AIMS maturity** — how operationalised the AIMS is beyond the documented policy.
- **Organisation-specific model** — a maturity rubric the function defined with the CAE and the audit committee's input.

### Posture

- Maturity trends are presented as the audit function's assessment with the methodology shown. The committee can agree, disagree, or request a different rubric.
- Where the ERM / governance function has its own maturity assessment, the audit function presents its view alongside; divergences are explained.
- Maturity is not an absolute score; the trend is the signal. A static or declining maturity trend is as important as an improving one.

## Reporting to the board of directors

Where the audit committee reports up to the full board, the AI audit function's significant content flows upward in a condensed shape.

### What the board usually sees

- The chair's summary of the audit committee's meetings.
- The audit function's annual report (via the committee).
- Escalations the audit committee has decided to raise to the full board.
- Director briefings on specific AI topics where the function has subject-matter expertise.

### Posture

The audit function does not usually report directly to the board routinely; the audit committee is the function's channel. Direct board engagement happens when the committee decides it is needed — a significant incident, a material unresolved disagreement with management, or a specific subject-matter briefing.

## The AI governance leader as a reporting audience

The organisation's head of AI governance (the senior role covered at level 60 in this curriculum ecosystem) is a legitimate reporting audience for the internal AI audit function — not as a supervisor but as a peer whose function the audit coordinates with.

### The coordination pattern

- The head of AI governance is often responsible for the first-line AI programme and the second-line AI risk function.
- The AI audit function is the third line and reports its findings on the head of AI governance's programme to the audit committee.
- The head of AI governance consumes audit findings as a counterpart; CAP commitments often fall to the head of AI governance's organisation.
- The head of AI governance is not an audit audience in the governance sense — the audit committee is — but is an operational counterpart to whom the function communicates findings, coordinates on continuous audit, and shares landscape intelligence.

### What the audit function does not do

- Report *to* the head of AI governance as a reporting line. The CAE reports to the audit committee; the AI audit function reports through the CAE.
- Let the head of AI governance review or approve audit findings before they go to the committee. Findings go to management (the finding's owner) for response under the function's engagement protocol; the head of AI governance's role in the response is determined by scope, not by reporting line.
- Treat the head of AI governance as the audit committee's voice on AI matters. The audit committee speaks for itself.

### What the audit function does do

- Shares continuous-audit dashboards and landscape intelligence with the head of AI governance where doing so improves the organisation's posture.
- Coordinates on major engagements so the head of AI governance's programme is not blind-sided and the audit's work is not duplicative.
- Receives the head of AI governance's view on AI risk and considers it as a data point, not as a governing input.
- Partners on landscape intelligence (new regulation, new incidents in the external environment) so the organisation moves coherently.

The clear line: the head of AI governance is a *counterpart*, not a *reporting line*. The function's reporting line remains the CAE and the audit committee.

## Executive-session dynamics

Many audit committees hold an executive session with the CAE at each meeting — private, without management, where candid concerns can be raised. For AI audit:

### Appropriate topics

- Management resistance to findings or CAPs.
- Resource constraints the CAE cannot solve inside the function.
- Concerns about management's candour with the committee.
- Concerns about independence, including subtle ones (reporting-line pressure; budget pressure).
- Concerns about the audit committee itself (missing expertise; cadence; attendance).

### What is not for executive session

- Material findings (these go in the regular agenda; executive session is not a secret channel for substantive issues).
- Rumour.
- Attacks on named individuals that do not rise to governance concern.

## Communication pitfalls

- **Over-scoping the finding presentation.** 25 findings presented in five minutes each — nothing lands. Prioritise the three or four that need the committee.
- **Technical-depth anchoring.** The AI content is technical; pulling the committee into the weeds costs their attention on the governance question. Use appendices.
- **Jargon.** "Guardrail false-positive rate drifted 0.2% quarter-over-quarter" is not a committee statement; "the system's safety filter is less effective, and here is what we are doing about it" is.
- **Over-certainty.** Quantifying effects to decimal places implies precision the finding cannot support.
- **Under-escalation.** Material matters delayed to the quarterly cadence; the committee hears about them too late to act.
- **Over-escalation.** Routine items pushed to the committee that belong at management; the committee's time is wasted.
- **Advocacy.** The function's role is to inform the committee, not to campaign on its behalf.

## A short rubric before the AI audit section goes in the audit-committee papers

- Can a non-AI-specialist board member understand the one-page summary?
- Does the dashboard reconcile to the audit's underlying records?
- Are findings presented at the scope of the committee's accountability (decisions, direction, challenge)?
- Are repeat findings surfaced explicitly, with structural cause?
- Is the maturity-model trend presented with methodology?
- Is the AI risk landscape updated for anything that happened since the prior pre-read?
- Is the decision log current?
- Is the pre-read consistent with the external auditor's planned communication (AS 1301 for US audits)?

## Summary

- The audit committee is the AI audit function's governance counterpart; the function's reporting shapes — pre-read, one-page summary, decision log, risk heatmap, dashboard, repeat-finding read, maturity-model trend — are the vehicles the committee uses to act.
- The one-page summary carries the committee's time-constrained read; every quarter it answers what the committee is being asked to do, what has happened, what to know, what is coming, and what the trend is.
- The dashboard presents plan execution, findings slate, repeat findings, continuous-audit exception trend, regulator engagement, external-auditor coordination, and resources — consistently quarter over quarter.
- The decision log tracks the committee's decisions and the function's follow-through, quarter to quarter.
- Repeat findings are the function's most important signal; they are surfaced explicitly with structural cause, prior CAP, and the committee's prior view.
- The maturity-model trend tells the longer story using NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, or an organisation-specific rubric; the methodology is presented so the committee can engage with the signal.
- The head of AI governance is a counterpart, not a reporting line; coordination preserves the function's independence while supporting organisational coherence.
- Executive sessions are reserved for governance concerns that cannot be handled in the regular agenda; material substantive findings are not held for private channels.
- Reporting pitfalls — over-scoping, technical anchoring, jargon, over-certainty, under- or over-escalation, advocacy — are the senior auditor's recurring discipline.
